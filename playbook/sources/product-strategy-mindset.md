
*A reference guide for building and defending product strategy*

---

## Section 1: Product Strategy

> Product strategy is about answering “Why?” and then hypothesising “How?”. It is not a roadmap, a set of metrics, or a list of features. It is the reasoning that connects what the business needs to what you choose to build, and it is your job to make sure the right voices and information are in the room to get there.

### What Product Strategy Is Not

Each of these is useful, and each gets mistaken for strategy. They are outputs of a strategy, or inputs to it, but none of them explain why you are doing what you are doing.

- Roadmaps
- Goals or metrics
- Features or products
- Budgets
- Mission statements

### Selecting What Is Most Valuable, Usable, Feasible and Viable

Strategy means choosing the items that score highest across all four risks. Each has a natural lead, and each fails in a recognisable way when it is ignored.

| Risk | Lead | Key question | If missed |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Valuable** | PRODUCT LEADS | Will customers buy it, or choose to use it? | If missed: nobody wants it. The reason a launch does nothing. |
| **Usable** | DESIGN LEADS | Can users work out how to use it, and want to? | If missed: they want it, but can't get through it. Drop-offs, contact-centre calls, workarounds. |
| **Feasible** | ENGINEERING LEADS | Can we build it with the time, skills and technology we have? | If missed: it works on the slide and not on the platform. Late, cut down, or never. |
| **Viable** | PRODUCT LEADS | Does it work for the business: risk, compliance, legal, finance, operations? | If missed: in regulated industries this one carries the weight. Regulation, risk appetite, governance. |

### Validating Value: Who + What + How

Value is only validated when all three line up. Miss one and you are guessing.

| WHO | WHAT | HOW |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Customer segments that have problems you can solve, and want what you can offer. | The process and impact of the solutions and features you deliver to solve those problems. | The hypotheses and experiments that confirm requirements and priority. |

## Section 2: Strategy Template

> Structure helps you see the wood for the trees. A strategy brief template isn't bureaucracy; it is a way of organising what you know so the gaps become obvious. If you can't clearly communicate your strategy and how it will move the goals, no one will support it.

### The Brief, Section by Section

| Section | Key questions | Purpose |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Why** | Why is this happening? Why is it needed? What has happened up to this point? | Covers the purpose of the document and its context. A powerful place to frame the strategy and call out the agreed goals, or the lack of them. |
| **Who** | Who is this strategy meant to serve, and what problems do they have? | Prioritises which user you are focused on, and gets ahead of the “what about…” questions from stakeholders. |
| **What** | What is most important to do now, and first? What will most impact our goals? | The most crucial section. Line up the data that validates the problem and supports your priority. No data, no reason to do it. |
| **How** | How will we proceed and get from here to there? | Lay out what you will build and tie it to the metrics you believe will move the organisation's objectives. The meat and potatoes, but you can't skip the middle to get here. |

## Section 3: Goals Alignment

> Two questions sit underneath every strategy: what does the business need to achieve, and why does the organisation fund your team? Good goals answer both, and they focus on outcomes, not outputs. This section covers the difference between business goals and product outcomes, how to sharpen goals, and one approach for aligning teams around them.

### Anatomy of an Outcome: Five Parts

A good goal is specific, measurable and actionable. Writing it in five parts makes all three unavoidable.

- **01 — Metric**
- **02 — Baseline**
- **03 — Target**
- **04 — Time horizon**
- **05 — Guardrails**

### Cascading Goals in an Organisation

There are lots of different kinds of goals, and they nest. Each level should clearly support the one above it.

- **Org goals:** what the organisation needs to accomplish to survive and thrive.
- **Product goals:** what the product can do to contribute to the org goals. Why they fund you.
- **Customer outcomes:** what the customer achieves thanks to your product.
- **Initiative goals:** what the larger programme can accomplish.
- **Feature goals:** what the feature should accomplish.
- **Metrics:** how you know the feature, initiative or goal is moving in the right direction.

```text
                        Org goal
                            ↓
                Product goal    Customer outcome
                            ↓
          Feature goal   Feature goal   Feature goal
                            ↓
                          Metric
                            ↓
                         Solution
```

### Facilitating a Goals Alignment Session

Use the session to educate your stakeholders. Get everything on the table first, sorted into three visible categories. Once it is all down, you have the power to organise it.

| Solutions | Customer outcomes | Org objectives |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Most often what stakeholders bring. Pick one up and track backwards: what outcome would this drive for our business or customer? | Where you want the conversation to focus. If it stalls, frame the problem the solution is trying to solve. | Stakeholders usually put solutions or metrics here. Your job is to help them see that. |

Your role is to help your stakeholders understand when something is a solution, think through what would a successful outcome for a customer would be, and reorganize the information so that there is a clear structure.

### When “We Don't Have Clear Goals”

- Answer “Why does my company fund my team?” It points to the value you should be driving.
- Don't stress about crafting the perfect customer-value goal. Pick one that is impactful for your team.
- Use an example to learn the basics, then brainstorm with your team how to apply it to your product.

## Section 4: Problem

> There is no point unless there is a problem. Most work arrives as a solution; turning it back into a problem statement is what lets you test whether it is worth doing at all.

### Write the Problem Statement

Fill in the blanks. Then write down how you know the problem exists.

> **[who]** struggles to **[do what]** when **[moment or situation]**, and we see it in **[observable signal]**.

## Section 5: Data & Insights

> Your data should tell a story, although it might not be the one you want. If you don't back up your story with data, it's pure fiction.

### Quant + Qual Exposes the Context You Need

**Quantitative · the “what”**

- Identifies the problem, e.g. conversion is down
- Tests statistical significance
- Signals new opportunities
- Evolves into measurable metrics

**Qualitative · the “why” and “how”**

- Details behaviour, usage and emotion
- Patterns and themes emerge
- Helps formulate potential solutions
- Morphs into ongoing usability research

### Types of Questions

Match the question to the method. Generative research opens up the problem; evaluative research and experiments narrow down the answer.

| Method | Product | Market |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Generative research** | How can we solve this problem? What form should it take? How important is the design? What's the quickest solution? | Who is our customer? What are their pains? What job do they need done? Is our segment too broad? How do we find them? |
| **Evaluative research** | What do people like and dislike? Why do they do that with our product or service? | Are they really willing to pay? How much? How do we convince them to buy? |
| **Experiment** | Is this solution working? Are people using it? Which solution is better? How should we optimise? What is the minimum feature set? | How much will it cost to sell? Can we scale marketing? How should we prioritise? |

### Knowledge Audit: What Do We Actually Know?

Sort everything you know about the problem into three columns. Good data discovery is fast, specific, usable, targeted, flexible and collaborative. Then star the one item that would be most disastrous if it proved wrong: that is your riskiest assumption.

| What we KNOW | What we THINK we know | What we're CURIOUS about |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Evidence.** Data you can pull tomorrow, verbatims with a source, a test result. | **Assumptions.** Sounds like fact; isn't, yet. If a sticky gets debated for more than a minute, it lives here. | **Unknowns.** “We need to learn X” is a legitimate entry, and often the riskiest. |

## Section 6: Hypothesis

> Focusing on outcomes means focusing on users. Your riskiest assumption becomes a hypothesis, and the hypothesis becomes a test small enough to run in a fortnight, ideally without needing anybody's sign-off.

### The Test Matrix

Four steps, each with a question to weigh it. The example is from a healthcare app.

| Step | Example | Weigh it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **1 · Hypothesis** — *We believe that…* | IF cardiologists receive regular reports of patient heart rates, THEN they will send actionable recommendations to prevent heart attacks. | Critical level: low, medium, high? |
| **2 · Test** — *To verify that, we will…* | Collect heart rates from 5 patients by phone 3 times a week, chart the change over time, and email it to each patient's cardiologist. | Test cost? Confidence in data? |
| **3 · Metric** — *We will measure…* | How many doctors respond with a list of recommendations for their patients. | Time required to measure? |
| **4 · Validation** — *We are right if…* | 4 out of 5 doctors email recommendations that patients report as useful. | |

### What's Your Confidence?

Not all evidence is equal. Use this scale to be honest about how much weight a belief can carry.

| Confidence | Evidence category | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **0–1** | Self-conviction | Pitch deck; thematic support, aligns with vision and strategy |
| **2** | Estimates and plans | Back-of-the-envelope estimates; feasibility evals |
| **3** | Anecdotal evidence | Top sales requests; 1–3 interested customers; product data points; all competitors have it |
| **4–5** | Market data | Supported by surveys |
| **6–7** | Customer and user evidence | Strong patterns in qualitative data; successful usability tests; longitudinal user studies |
| **8–9** | Test results | Alpha/beta; A/B experiments |
| **10** | Launch and usage data | Platform data |

## Section 7: Iterations

> What we do not do is as important as what we do. Features are outputs; we want to achieve outcomes. A roadmap that tells a story of outcomes is far easier to defend than a list of features.

### Outcomes Tell a Story, Features Don't

| Instead of features | Try outcomes |
| --- | --- |
| Sign-in with Google and Facebook | Reduce friction in the sign-up process |
| Year-end summary of books I've read | Motivate users to add more books to their bookshelf |
| Add “Stories” functionality | Increase engagement from “lurker” users who view but don't post |
| Emoji reactions | Let users express themselves |

### Ways to Structure a Roadmap

Pick the structure that best tells your story. Then stack-rank items by your confidence in their ability to create value, and be ready to defend that order.

- **Now · Next · Later**
- **Must · Should · Could have**
- **Crawl · Walk · Run**
- **Bones · Muscle · Brain**
